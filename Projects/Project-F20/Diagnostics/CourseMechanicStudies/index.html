01 / Autonomous course mechanic

Iron Tide

A top-view factory greybox for testing how overhead gantry cranes, moving magnetic fields, vehicle construction and tyre grip continuously rewrite the production-floor route.

Saved visual research Facility reference images 04 images

These references establish the intended realism: unmarked movement corridors created by production machinery, AGVs and transfer carts at floor level, and overhead bridge cranes creating pressure above them. They are reference material only and remain hidden during normal simulation use.

01

Mechanics

  1. Autonomous factory traffic. AGVs, transfer carts and coil carriers follow machine-defined aisles without player steering, so the magnetic system can be judged continuously rather than in a staged example.
  2. Orthogonal factory gantries. The whole bridge moves forward/back on fixed runways, then the trolley moves left/right across that bridge. Only one axis moves at a time, so every lift follows a readable L-shaped route rather than cutting diagonally across the factory.
  3. Grip versus pull. Tyre friction resists lateral magnetic force. High vehicle-metal content increases attraction, making the same corner safe for one build and unstable for another.
  4. Live obstacles. I-beams, plates, drums, pipe bundles and broken machinery slide into traffic, collide with cars and can be dragged clear by the next crane.
  5. Material supply. Two inbound conveyors continually dump replacement steel into the factory. Gantries collect it, carry it across the route and release it into marked sorting zones.
  6. Mechanical failure. Heavy loads stress cables and trolley drives. Low power can release a load, an underspecified lift can snap its cable, and sustained high power can overload or drop an entire gantry.
  7. Rare crane collisions. Bridge-crane proximity control normally makes one gantry yield. A very rare sensor outage removes that protection, allowing two moving bridges to collide, drop their loads and shut down temporarily.

02

Features

  • Top-view factory concept-art / greybox presentation
  • Unmarked traffic aisles formed by machine bays, conveyors, pipe runs and stored material
  • One to six independently travelling orthogonal-axis gantries
  • Automated inbound steel conveyors and four staggered material drop zones
  • Visible power-loss, cable-snap, overload, gantry-drop and rare collision failures
  • Multiple autonomous material-handling vehicles in both directions
  • Material-dependent magnetic attraction
  • Friction-limited tyre correction and visible skids
  • Movable scrap with chain-reaction collisions
  • Combined magnetic fields where crane ranges overlap

03

Ideas

  • Vehicle construction as difficulty. Let the player choose a light composite vehicle, a steel-bodied carrier, or a heavily loaded coil transporter. More metal improves durability and cargo value but increases magnetic pull.
  • Read the factory cycle. Safe movement comes from watching bridge travel, trolley travel, feeder dumps and AGV queues—then crossing during a polarity gap or while a gantry is committed to a sorting bay.
  • Orthogonal crane routing. Bridge cranes move forward/back first and trolleys move left/right second. This makes their intention readable while still creating intersections, waiting, deadlocks and rare collisions.
  • Dynamic sorting contracts. Change which material belongs in each drop zone. Incorrectly sorted steel is returned to the floor, while a clean batch temporarily reduces debris density.
  • Cargo versus control. Optional steel cargo increases score and can unlock heavy machinery, but every collected kilogram makes the player more vulnerable to active magnetic fields.
  • Failure cascades. A snapped cable creates a new obstacle; that obstacle can block an AGV, force another crane to reroute and overload a neighbouring sorting bay.
  • Connect the studies. Delivered scrap can become ballast for Dead Weight, turning performance in the factory into a physical advantage on the following bridge mechanic.

Dead Weight

01

Mechanics

  1. Two real alternatives. Both bridge mechanisms are shown as side elevations placed side by side. Neither is presented as an upper route, and the deck angle remains directly visible throughout the crossing.
  2. Torque selects the level. Vehicle mass and distance from the central pivot create load torque. Counterweight leverage supplies the opposing moment, latching the deck to Level 3 above, Level 2 straight ahead or Level 1 below.
  3. Three genuinely different destinations. Level 3 is an upper crane-service road with exposed lifting operations; Level 2 is the main material-transfer floor with dense AGV traffic; Level 1 is a lower maintenance sublevel with pipes, low clearance and accumulated debris. The exits are separate roads, not three labels for the same route.
  4. Two route calculations. Each bridge has different counterweight geometry, so Bridge A and Bridge B can connect to different levels under the same vehicle. Before every run, the vehicle selects the safer bridge and follows its highlighted exit.
  5. Gravity becomes traffic. Loose precast concrete barriers accelerate toward the low end whenever a deck tilts. Vehicles climbing and descending the same span must change lane around barriers moving in the opposite direction.
  6. Failure remains physical. A badly overloaded deck misses its landing, pitches beyond the latch angle, removes tyre grip and slides the vehicle back to the checkpoint rather than simply declaring the choice invalid.

02

Features

  • Two adjacent side-elevation switching bridges
  • Three visible exit paths per bridge: Levels 1, 2 and 3
  • Distinct upper service road, central transfer floor and lower maintenance sublevel
  • Live angle readouts and visibly rotating decks
  • Highlighted landing path for each counterweight setup
  • Gravity-driven concrete barriers with visible slide direction
  • Alternating uphill and downhill autonomous crossings
  • Automatic barrier avoidance demonstration
  • Live vehicle-mass and leverage controls
  • Automatic bridge and level comparison before every crossing
  • Visible pivots, counterweight carriages and torque arms
  • Deck pitch, missed landings, tyre slip and checkpoint recovery

03

Ideas

  • Mass is a route key. Optional cargo can make one bridge safer and the other impossible, so collecting an upgrade changes the route rather than only changing a statistic.
  • Remote preparation. Counterweight carriages can be moved by machinery elsewhere in the level before the player reaches the crossing, rewarding observation and planning.
  • Convoy sequencing. Several autonomous carriers can enter in order. A light scout stabilizes the first span, a loaded transporter changes its pitch, and the player chooses when to join.
  • Recoverable failure. An overloaded deck does not simply switch off. It pitches, removes tyre grip, slides the vehicle back and leaves the bridge oscillating for the next attempt.
  • Shared resources. Loose magnetic scrap delivered from Iron Tide becomes temporary ballast, directly linking the factory sorting result to bridge stability.
  • Level-specific continuation. Level 3 can feed into suspended crane traffic, Level 2 into crowded production cells and Level 1 into a constrained pipe-and-maintenance network. The bridge choice therefore determines the next full sublevel and its mechanic.
  • Barrier management. Players can deliberately tilt an empty bridge to clear concrete blocks onto a collection apron, or leave them loose and force opponents and returning traffic to change lanes through a moving obstacle field.
  • Late-run reversal. A moving counterweight can cross the pivot while the vehicle is on the deck, changing both the connected level and the direction in which every loose barrier slides.

03 / Travelling surface mechanic

Breaker Wave

A real-time three-dimensional barrel wave tapered toward one side. Its break sweeps across the water, so the vehicle must hold a diagonal escape line instead of travelling straight into the closing lip.

Parameters

01

Mechanics

  1. A moving, one-sided taper. A tall standing wall curls into a thick hooked crest above a broad water floor. The open shoulder narrows down-course, while travelling deformation and crest spray make it read as a breaking ocean wave rather than a circular tunnel.
  2. The vehicle begins inside. Before the ride starts, the vehicle is already held against the lower inside face so the relationship between tyres, water and the escape line is immediately clear.
  3. The route must be diagonal. The vehicle needs enough traverse angle to move across the wave face toward the open shoulder. A straight line remains inside the moving closure and is swallowed; an excessively steep line loses forward support.
  4. Water-running tyres. Buoyant tyre vanes generate support only while relative speed is high enough. Grip and speed determine whether the vehicle can hold its selected angle while climbing the face.
  5. Closure creates the deadline. The lip sweeps from the wide side toward the taper. The vehicle must reach the shoulder before that moving break intersects its line.
  6. Failure returns to a checkpoint. Lost tyre support or a swallowed line is identified in the live readout, then the ride can restart inside the following wave section.

02

Features

  • Raw WebGL three-dimensional wave geometry
  • Asymmetrical standing wall, horizontal water floor and hooked overhang
  • Barrel that visibly tapers toward its open shoulder
  • Longitudinal motion and a break sweeping across the wave face
  • Multi-frequency surface noise, reflections and turbulence
  • Dense animated spray tracing the moving crest
  • Vehicle held inside the wave before starting
  • Selectable diagonal traverse line
  • Water-running tyre grip, speed and angle relationship
  • Distinct clear, swallowed and lost-support states
  • User-triggered low-frequency water roar

03

Ideas

  • Angle trade-off. A steeper traverse reaches the open shoulder sooner but sheds forward water speed; a shallow traverse preserves support while giving the sweeping lip more time to catch the vehicle.
  • Surface-reading challenge. Dark bands, foam and reflected factory lights can reveal fast water, dead patches and turbulence before the tyres reach them.
  • Variable exits. Some wave sections can open toward a dry ramp, others into a second barrel, and others onto a temporary floating service deck.
  • Carry consequences forward. Heavy steel cargo from Iron Tide improves stability in crosswind but requires more speed to remain on the water surface.
  • Mechanical tyre damage. Contact with suspended debris can break individual tyre vanes, lowering support on one side and forcing a corrective wall climb.
  • Wave trains. Missing one exit need not mean instant failure; the player can drop into the trough and attempt to enter the following barrel from below.

04 / Conveyor dynamics mechanic

Beltline

A straight transfer course where conveyor velocity, wheel speed and surface construction determine motion while upper, lower, front and back feeders push industrial freight through the vehicle’s line.

Drive setup

01

Mechanics

  1. All three sections and the wheels are independent. Ribbed rubber, wet steel and free rollers each have their own commanded velocity from reverse to 180 km/h. Ground speed is the physical result of the active section, driven wheels and available friction.
  2. Every surface transfers motion differently. Ribbed rubber grips hard and can launch the carrier, wet steel saturates early and produces long slides, while free rollers transfer almost no belt speed.
  3. Sections change suddenly and independently. The live zones take turns reversing against their commanded direction while a run is active—the green rubber section can abruptly run backward while the other two continue forward.
  4. Steering still needs grip. The steering-line input requests a lane, but lateral acceleration is limited by the current surface. A carrier sliding on wet steel cannot instantly reach the selected line.
  5. Feeders enter from every axis. Upper and lower transfer chutes cross the course, while front and back feeds send freight with and against the running line. Mass, velocity and collision shape alter the carrier rather than acting as visual obstacles.
  6. Outcomes remain physical. Excess slip, a high-grip launch, freight impacts, departure over either edge and crossing the finish are all consequences of the same motion model.

02

Features

  • Three independent reverse-to-180 km/h section inputs
  • Independent driven-wheel velocity
  • Visible autonomous reversals cycling through every section
  • Ribbed rubber, wet steel and free-roller zones
  • Upper, lower, front and back freight feeders
  • Crates, drums and long steel beams
  • Live lane steering with grip-limited response
  • Freight-to-vehicle collision impulses
  • Sliding, launch, impact, edge-departure and finish states
  • Run and checkpoint reset control

03

Ideas

  • Read the seams. Surface labels and belt streaks telegraph the next transfer law, but the exact speed step is only revealed when that section changes.
  • Use reverse belts defensively. A reversed zone can hold the carrier away from crossing freight, then become a launch pad when wheel speed overcomes it.
  • Move freight deliberately. A glancing impact can push a crate into another lane or rotate a beam until it bridges two surface sections.
  • Chain the studies. Iron Tide cranes can determine feeder loads, while Dead Weight cargo mass can change Beltline impact resistance and launch behaviour.
  • Damage the transfer line. Heavy beam strikes could jam free rollers or strip rubber ribs, permanently changing friction for following vehicles.
  • Branch at the finish. Successful exit velocity can determine which elevated conveyor, loading bay or service floor the carrier reaches next.

Beachhead

01

Mechanics

  1. Automated systems own the field. Uncrewed mechanical emplacements sweep the coast without depicting soldiers, human targets or a historical battle.
  2. Fire patterns are readable systems. Lane bursts concentrate tracers into one approach channel; crossing fire draws opposing diagonal paths. Mechanical sweep motion telegraphs where each pattern moves next.
  3. Impacts warn before they land. Contracting circles show location and remaining time. Detonation damages nearby vehicles and leaves a crater that persists for the rest of the run.
  4. The route physically weaves. Alternating concrete barricades, block walls and fortified trench mouths force the rover through a visible slalom. Its steering target follows those openings instead of driving up a straight centre line.
  5. Tank mines form real minefields. Rows of rendered anti-vehicle pressure mines preserve one shifting safe corridor. Contact detonates the mine, throws the rover laterally, removes integrity and leaves another persistent crater.
  6. Smoke is a timed physical resource. A deployed cloud expands from the rover, obscures automated tracer acquisition and displaces predicted impact targeting until it disperses.
  7. Configuration changes the solution. The light rover turns and accelerates quickly but has less integrity; the heavy rover absorbs impacts and crosses crater rims but changes lanes slowly.

02

Features

  • Original hand-rendered coastal field with no historical recreation
  • Dense illustrated terrain, shoreline, trenches, walls, mines and damage
  • Vehicle-only conflict with no human targets
  • Automated tracer sweep geometry
  • Alternating burst-lane and crossing-fire patterns
  • Winding fortified trenches with sandbag and timber construction
  • Segmented concrete walls and barricade approach channels
  • Dense anti-vehicle minefields with physical detonations
  • Autonomous slalom route through shifting safe gaps
  • Contracting explosive warning circles
  • Persistent run-specific craters
  • User-deployable expanding smoke
  • Light and heavy rover configurations
  • Integrity, extraction and recovery states

03

Ideas

  • Pattern composition. Later fields can layer a slow mechanical sweep over short lane bursts, creating moving safe pockets rather than random unavoidable damage.
  • Crater routing. Heavy vehicles can use shallow crater rims as cover while light vehicles must route around the traction loss inside them.
  • Smoke interaction. Crosswind can push smoke into a neighbouring channel, protecting a later vehicle while exposing the rover that deployed it.
  • False telegraphs. Damaged automated emplacements can paint warning circles that fail to detonate, testing whether the player trusts the machinery.
  • Freight consequences. Beltline beams can become barricades and Iron Tide scrap can alter armour mass without introducing infantry or historical iconography.
  • Multiple extraction gates. Exit integrity and configuration can route the rover toward repair, cargo transfer or a faster inland conveyor.

01

Mechanics

  1. The permanent way is the controller. Unit A7 remains autonomous; powered decks physically lift occupied rail segments between the infeed, upper transfer line and outfeed.
  2. Momentum survives transfer. Longitudinal velocity remains on a laterally accelerating deck. Brake pressure must dissipate mass-dependent momentum before wheels reach an open seam.
  3. The rotary bridge is a physical switch. At 0° it completes the upper main line. At 42° it feeds a maintenance spur. Between index points, the next rail simply is not there.
  4. Timing determines traffic. Offset changes Table B, rotary indexing and the service carrier’s phase. A safe vehicle can wait, enter a wrong spur or meet crossing traffic after one timing change.
  5. Congestion occupies space. Slab, battery and service carriers operate continuously. A7 yields only while its available braking distance remains greater than its current stopping distance.
  6. Every result is physical. Aligned transfer, seam side-slip, wrong-spur routing, platform collision and successful exit follow from position, velocity, orientation and contact.

02

Features

  • Expanded full-width heavy-industry scene
  • Single consistent detailed top-down projection
  • Two 80-ton lateral transfer structures
  • Indexed rotating rail bridge and two dead-end service spurs
  • Guide racks, rollers, cable chains, drive motors and deck bogies
  • Workshops, pipe racks, drainage, fencing and freight storage
  • Three autonomous congestion vehicles
  • Mass-dependent stopping distance and visible braking trails
  • Live signals, machinery state and dispatch outcome
  • Aligned, side-slip, wrong-spur, collision and exit states

03

Ideas

  • Queue pressure. A second loaded carrier can arrive behind A7 and remove the option to wait indefinitely for the next deck cycle.
  • Brake heat. Repeated heavy stops can fade platform brakes, making a previously safe setup fail deeper in the hall.
  • Asymmetric freight. Offset cargo can unload one rail as the deck accelerates sideways, creating flange climb without changing total mass.
  • Persistent damage. A collision can bend a rail nose and leave a measurable alignment error for every following carrier.
  • Destination contracts. A maintenance-spur dispatch can make the apparent wrong route the required delivery for specialist cargo.
  • Connected freight. Beltline cargo and Iron Tide steel can set A7’s mass and braking demand before depot entry.

Vehicle and arena physics

Heat configuration

Drive directly during the heat. These physical inputs alter collision authority, stopping distance, tyre loading and the arena's pursuit rate.

01

Mechanics

  1. The player races directly. Throttle, braking and steering remain live throughout the heat. Rivals follow the same physical surfaces and can be rammed, blocked or forced onto damaged structure.
  2. Every ring compresses the field. Five concentric roads become shorter, tighter and narrower toward the centre. Surviving vehicles repeatedly rejoin from radial crossings into denser traffic.
  3. The pursuer is permanent structural failure. Arena sectors fracture, tilt and fall in sequence behind the pack. Missing a transfer gate means another partial lap with less road remaining.
  4. Crossings remember impact. Each radial span consists of independently loaded plates. Combined mass, impact velocity and collision impulses create visible deflection and permanent fractures.
  5. Gates force commitment. A gate can be open, closing or locked. Braking early surrenders position; arriving late means impact, congestion or another lap in front of the collapse.
  6. Mass creates a real trade-off. Heavy vehicles dominate contact but damage spans and stop late. Light vehicles preserve plates but can be displaced by rivals and falling debris.

02

Features

  • Direct keyboard, pointer and controller-style driving inputs
  • Twenty-four simultaneous race vehicles
  • Five progressively tighter concentric racing rings
  • Timed radial transfer gates
  • Load-sensitive multi-plate crossings
  • Persistent cracks, deflection and structural collapse
  • Advancing wedge-shaped pursuit failure
  • Physical player-versus-rival contact
  • Falling debris and permanently removed road sectors
  • Visible central finish from the starting ring

03

Ideas

  • Qualifying variants. Reverse the gate sequence or begin on two outer rings that merge into one elimination field.
  • Team betrayal. Team mass can hold a damaged plate level, but the first vehicle to leave may collapse the crossing under everyone behind.
  • Vehicle damage. Gate impacts can bend steering, reduce tyre grip or leave bodywork as persistent debris.
  • Dynamic finish. The central span can rotate between final-ring entrances, forcing the last survivors into one more positional fight.
  • Falling upper structure. Eliminated outer-ring sections can land across lower rings as temporary ramps or obstructions.
  • Twenty-four-player heat. Networked rivals can replace simulated cars without changing structural or vehicle physics.

Vehicle and signal physics

Heat configuration

Drive directly. Mass, torque, braking and grip determine how much momentum remains when the warning expires.

01

Mechanics

  1. The player races directly. Throttle, steering and braking remain fully controllable. The finish is always forward; every mandatory stop costs real race position.
  2. The warning tests stopping distance. Mass, road speed, tyre grip, brake pressure and accumulated heat determine whether the vehicle can become stationary before judgement.
  3. The scanner reads motion, not input. Releasing the throttle is insufficient. Wheel speed, downhill coast, lateral sliding and collision impulse are all measured when the curtain crosses the car.
  4. Other racers remain physical. A stopped vehicle can be rear-ended and pushed back above the motion threshold. Drivers must consider where they stop as well as when.
  5. Elimination changes the road. A detected vehicle's support plate unlocks and tilts it out of the heat, leaving a temporary opening and debris for racers behind.
  6. Brakes retain heat. Repeated emergency stops reduce available pressure. A setup that survives the first signal may require earlier braking during the final sector.

02

Features

  • Direct keyboard and pointer driving
  • Twenty-four simultaneous race vehicles
  • Large camera-followed monumental causeway
  • Open, warning, scan and release signal phases
  • Descending physical scan curtain
  • Momentum-based movement judgement
  • Rival reaction times and braking failures
  • Rear impacts during mandatory stops
  • Brake temperature and fade
  • Tilting road-plate eliminations

03

Ideas

  • False warning. Some signals can begin an amber phase and return to open, punishing drivers who surrender speed too early.
  • Multiple curtains. Later heats can scan front-to-back and back-to-front simultaneously, leaving a narrow moving safe region.
  • Draft pressure. Slipstreaming improves acceleration but gives the following driver less visibility and braking distance.
  • Damaged brakes. Contact with lane walls or release plates can permanently bias braking to one side.
  • Signal camouflage. Dust and eliminated vehicles can obscure low-mounted warning lamps without hiding the primary overhead signal.
  • Final judgement. The last scan can begin with the finish already visible, forcing leaders to choose between stopping safely and attempting to finish before the curtain arrives.

Vehicle and course physics

Compression configuration

Drive directly. Vehicle setup determines whether the car can change lanes under load, absorb contact and escape a moving wall.

01

Mechanics

  1. The player drives the entire race. Throttle, braking and steering remain available from the eight-lane launch to the single-lane finish.
  2. The pack defines safety. A checkpoint census counts the physical lane position of every active vehicle. The least-occupied lane is selected for removal rather than chosen arbitrarily.
  3. Selection creates a warning window. Edge lamps and surface markings identify the losing strip before its locks release, giving drivers a brief chance to merge.
  4. Sidewalls perform the merge. Both boundaries move inward through each chamber. Cars can be pushed, pinned against rivals or carried toward the opening left by the peeled lane.
  5. Occupancy is strategic. Entering an unpopular lane may save it during the census. Leaving immediately afterward can condemn isolated rivals without sacrificing the player's route.
  6. The road remembers every checkpoint. One lane is permanently removed after each chamber. Seven physical compressions reduce the course from eight lanes to one before the finish.

02

Features

  • Direct keyboard and pointer driving
  • Twenty-four physical race vehicles
  • Seven pack-counted compression checkpoints
  • Eight-to-one persistent course narrowing
